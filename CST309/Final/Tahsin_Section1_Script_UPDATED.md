## SCRIPT

**[0:00–0:35 | ON CAMERA]**

"What does it mean to be a citizen of the world when the state you live in refuses to see you as a citizen at home?

In political theory, we are taught that citizenship is the ultimate guarantee of human dignity — a legal shield protecting our basic rights. But across modern history, millions of human beings have discovered a terrifying truth: that when you fall between the arbitrary borders of sovereign states, your fundamental human rights evaporate into thin air."

**[0:35–1:15 | ON CAMERA — quote card on screen]** **[FIXED: replaced invented quote with Nussbaum's actual wording]**

"In her 1994 essay *Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism*, philosopher Martha Nussbaum argued that nationality itself is, in her words, a **'morally irrelevant characteristic'** (Nussbaum, 1994) — that where you happen to be born is an accident of birth, not a measure of your worth as a human being. For Nussbaum, our primary moral allegiance should belong not to the nation-state, but to the worldwide community of human beings.

Yet our global order remains strictly bound by state borders. And nowhere is the tragedy of this state-centric system clearer than right here in Bangladesh — in the contested, protracted statelessness of the Urdu-speaking Bihari community."

**[1:15–2:05 | VOICEOVER — archival montage]** **[FIXED: added 1972 population figure and 2003 ruling]**

"The story of the Biharis is a story of double displacement. In 1947, fleeing communal violence during the Partition of British India, hundreds of thousands of Urdu-speaking Muslims migrated from Bihar to East Bengal, arriving as recognized Pakistani citizens.

But when Bangladesh won its independence in the 1971 Liberation War, the ground shattered beneath their feet. Branded collectively as collaborators, an estimated 900,000 Urdu speakers were left without citizenship under the 1972 Citizenship Order after Pakistan refused to repatriate them (Hussain, 2009). It would take three more decades — and a first, partial legal opening in 2003 — before the courts began to reverse this exclusion (Hussain, 2009)."

**[2:05–2:45 | VOICEOVER — B-roll of camp alleys]** **[FIXED: added page numbers; reconciled camp count with Section 2 to avoid contradicting Arnab's figures]**

"As anthropologist Dina Siddiqi documents in her 2013 study *Left Behind by the Nation*, the UNHCR refused them refugee status because they had not crossed an international border after 1971 (Siddiqi, 2013, p. 150). Trapped in an institutional black hole — neither refugees nor citizens — hundreds of thousands were forced into what Siddiqi calls decades-long 'temporary' settlements (Siddiqi, 2013, p. 164), settlements that Arnab will show you have now lasted over fifty years (Siddiqi, 2013, p. 166)."

**[2:45–3:30 | ON CAMERA — Anderson graphic]**

"To understand why this exclusion persisted for decades, we turn to Benedict Anderson's theory of imagined communities. Anderson showed that modern nations are constructed political communities, built through language and shared print culture (Anderson, 1983).

Post-1971 Bangladeshi nation-building defined its identity around Bengali language and ethnicity — nationality built on *ethnos* rather than *demos*. To define what it meant to be Bengali, the state needed an Other — and the Urdu-speaking Bihari became the permanent insider-alien within the national landscape."

**[3:30–4:15 | VOICEOVER — split-screen Walzer/Linklater graphic]** **[FIXED: Walzer now has a citation]**

"Communitarian thinkers like Michael Walzer argue that membership is the primary good a political community distributes, and that citizenship only has meaning inside a bounded national community (Walzer, 1983). But political theorist Andrew Linklater challenges this in his 2002 work *Cosmopolitan Citizenship*.

Linklater argues that when states tie rights strictly to national membership, vulnerable internal minorities are left unprotected (Linklater, 2002). The Bihari experience proves his point: formal sovereignty without cosmopolitan harm-conventions creates spaces of total rightlessness — what Hannah Arendt famously called the loss of 'the right to have rights' (Arendt, 1968)."

**[4:15–5:00 | VOICEOVER — 2008 judgment / NID / rejected passport montage]**

"In May 2008, the High Court Division delivered the *Abid Khan* ruling, recognizing that Urdu speakers born after 1971 are birthright Bangladeshi citizens (Hussain, 2009). Statelessness was legally abolished on paper.

But as Hussain (2009) and sociologist Victoria Redclift (2013) both show, a gap endures between formal citizenship and lived citizenship. Bihari youth hold National ID cards and vote in elections, yet passport and job applications are routinely rejected the moment their address lists a camp."

**[5:00–5:35 | ON CAMERA — handoff]**

"Winning legal recognition on paper in 2008 was a real achievement. But as long as a camp address functions as an invisible border of exclusion, cosmopolitan inclusion — the kind Nussbaum and Linklater both argue for — remains unfinished. That's exactly why this is a *global* citizenship problem, not just a domestic legal footnote.

To see what that gap looks like on the ground, I hand over to my teammate Arnab, who takes us inside the camps."

### APA 7 formatting notes (why each italic is where it is)
- **Book titles** (Anderson, Walzer) are italicized in full.
- **Journal titles** are italicized (*Forced Migration Review*, *Asian Affairs*, *Sites*, *Boston Review*), and the **volume number** immediately after it is also italicized — the issue number in parentheses is *not* italicized. Example: *Forced Migration Review*, *32*, 30–31.
- **Chapter titles** (Linklater, Arendt) are NOT italicized; only the **edited book title** they appear in is italicized.
- No "Retrieved from" language before URLs — APA 7 just gives the URL directly.
