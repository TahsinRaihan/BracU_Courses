# 1. BENEDICT ANDERSON — *Imagined Communities* (Intro + Ch.3)

## Context and stakes
- Written against the background of the Vietnam–Cambodia–China wars (1978–79) — Marxist states fighting each other. This is Anderson's puzzle: why would ostensibly internationalist, class-based revolutionary regimes fight along *national* lines?
- His target: Marxist theory has never adequately explained nationalism. It treats nationalism as an embarrassing "anomaly" rather than confronting it head-on. Marx never explained why "each country" in "the proletariat of each country" mattered theoretically.
- Anderson proposes a "Copernican" shift: don't ask what's "wrong" with nationalism (comparing it to supposedly more coherent ideologies like liberalism or fascism); instead treat it anthropologically, like kinship or religion.

## Three paradoxes of nationalism that confound theorists
1. **Objective modernity vs. subjective antiquity** — historians know nations are modern, but nationalists themselves feel their nation is ancient/eternal.
2. **Formal universality vs. concrete particularity** — everyone "has" a nationality the way they "have" a gender (it's a universal category), yet each specific nationality (e.g., "Greek-ness") is treated as utterly unique and non-transferable.
3. **Political power vs. philosophical poverty** — nationalism has moved millions to kill and die, yet it has produced no major systematic thinkers (no nationalist Hobbes, Tocqueville, Marx, or Weber). It is "empty" as a philosophy but massively powerful as a force.

## Anderson's core definition of the nation
The nation is **an imagined political community, imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign.**

Breaking down each term — this is likely the single most quotable/citable definitional passage from the whole reading list:

- **IMAGINED**: because members of even the smallest nation will never know, meet, or even hear of most of their fellow members — yet each carries a mental image of their communion/connection. This is not the same as "false" — Anderson explicitly pushes back against Gellner's framing that nationalism is invention-as-fabrication/falsity. Instead, *all* communities beyond small face-to-face villages are imagined; the real question is not true vs. false community but the **style** in which a community is imagined (e.g., particularistic kinship-based imagining vs. national imagining).
- **LIMITED**: because even the largest nation has finite (if elastic) boundaries beyond which other nations exist. No nation — even the most messianic — imagines itself coextensive with all of humanity, unlike, say, universalizing religious visions of a wholly Christian planet.
- **SOVEREIGN**: because the concept of "nation" was born in the Enlightenment/Revolutionary era, precisely as the legitimacy of divinely-ordained hierarchical dynastic realms was collapsing. Nations dream of being free — the emblem of this freedom is the sovereign state.
- **COMMUNITY**: because regardless of actual inequality/exploitation within it, the nation is always conceived as a deep, horizontal *comradeship* — a fraternity. This fraternity is what makes it possible for so many people to be willing not just to kill, but to *die*, for something so relatively recently invented and imaginatively limited.

## Key supporting points from the Introduction
- Renan's famous point (cited by Anderson): the essence of a nation is that all its individuals have much in common — *and* that they have all forgotten many things (collective forgetting/amnesia is constitutive of national identity, not incidental).
- Gellner's claim that nationalism "invents nations where they do not exist" is useful but flawed for Anderson because Gellner conflates "invention" with "fabrication" — implying there's some other, more "true," non-invented community out there to contrast nations against. Anderson rejects this; imagining is not lying.
- Example: Javanese villagers always knew they were connected to people they'd never seen, but this connection was imagined *particularistically* (as stretchable kinship/clientship networks), not nationally, and the Javanese language historically had no word for "society" as an abstraction.
- Example: the French aristocracy under the *ancien régime* is retrospectively imagined by us as a "class," but this is anachronistic — historically, identity was reckoned through personal/genealogical ties ("the lord of X," "the uncle of the Baronne de Y") not class abstraction. Class-based imagining came late.

## Chapter 3: "The Origins of National Consciousness" — the print-capitalism argument
This is Anderson's causal/mechanistic explanation for **why** national imagining became possible and popular.

### The core thesis: convergence of three forces
National consciousness became possible through the explosive interaction of:
1. **Capitalism** (a system of production/productive relations)
2. **Print technology** (a communications technology)
3. **The fatality of human linguistic diversity** (an irreducible fact — languages cannot be unified, but they CAN be assembled into fewer print-languages)

This convergence is often shorthanded as **"print-capitalism."**

### Historical mechanics
- By 1500, at least 20 million books had already been printed in Europe; possibly 200 million by 1600. Francis Bacon said print had changed "the appearance and state of the world."
- Book publishing was one of capitalism's earliest enterprise forms — printers set up branches all over Europe, ignoring political borders, creating an early "international" of publishing houses driven by profit motive.
- **The Latin market saturated first.** Latin readers were a thin, elite, bilingual stratum. Once that market was exhausted (after ~150 years), capitalist logic pushed printers toward the far larger vernacular-speaking (monoglot) masses — because that's where the money was.

### Three extraneous factors that reinforced vernacularization
1. **Change in Latin's own character** — Renaissance Humanists' revival of classical Latin literature made written Latin increasingly Ciceronian, ornate, and removed from everyday/ecclesiastical use — making it *more* esoteric and arcane, accelerating its disconnection from ordinary people.
2. **The Reformation** — this is a major one. Before print, Rome had always won wars against heresy because it had superior internal lines of communication. But when Luther nailed his theses in 1517, they were printed in German translation and seen "within 15 days" all over the country. Luther became, in effect, history's first best-selling author — someone who could sell books on the strength of his name alone. Between 1522–1546, 430 editions of his Biblical translations appeared. Protestantism was always on the offensive because it exploited the expanding vernacular print market, while the Counter-Reformation defended the "citadel of Latin" (symbolized by the Vatican's *Index Librorum Prohibitorum*, which had no Protestant counterpart). Example: François I's panicked 1535 ban on printing any books in his realm (on pain of death) was unenforceable because his eastern borders were already ringed with Protestant print centers (Calvin's Geneva alone went from 42 editions published 1533–1540 to 527 editions 1550–1564).
3. **Administrative vernaculars / centralizing monarchs** — separate from print and Reformation, the slow rise of vernacular languages as tools of state administration (e.g., Norman French superseding Latin in England after the Conquest, then fusing with Anglo-Saxon into Early English, becoming the language of courts and Parliament after 1362; French becoming the official language of French courts of justice only in 1539 via the Edict of Villers-Cotterêts). Crucially, Anderson stresses this was pragmatic and unselfconscious — not proto-nationalist ideology, just administrative convenience.

### How print-languages built the foundation for national consciousness — THREE mechanisms
1. **Created unified fields of exchange/communication below Latin and above spoken vernaculars.** Speakers of mutually incomprehensible spoken dialects (many different spoken "Frenches" or "Englishes") became able to understand each other through print. This created an awareness of belonging to a specific language-field of hundreds of thousands/millions of "fellow-readers" — and awareness that *only* those people belonged to that field. This mutual, print-mediated, invisible-yet-felt connection is literally the embryo of national imagining.
2. **Gave language new *fixity*.** Print freezes language in a stable, endlessly reproducible form, unlike hand-copied manuscripts which drifted with each scribe's "unconsciously modernizing" habits. This fixity built up an image of linguistic antiquity — by the 17th century European languages had basically assumed their modern forms, creating the sense that "our" language has always existed, feeding the nation's self-image of deep antiquity.
3. **Created new languages-of-power with internal hierarchies.** Certain dialects were "closer" to the emerging print-standard and dominated it (e.g., "High German" became prestigious print-German while "Northwestern German" was demoted to sub-standard "Platt Deutsch," in a way that was NOT true of, e.g., Czech, which successfully resisted assimilation into print-German). This explains later "sub-national" struggles to get one's own vernacular into print/radio.

### Important qualifications
- Anderson repeatedly stresses this was **largely unselfconscious and unideological at the time** — no early modern actor was pursuing "national consciousness" as a project. The nationalist *use* of these processes (e.g., Atatürk's imposed romanization of Turkish to sever ties with wider Islamic/Arabic-script identification, or Stalin's forced Cyrillicization) came much later and was highly deliberate/Machiavellian — a different, second-order phenomenon from the original "fatal," semi-accidental convergence.
- Nation-states are NOT isomorphic with print-language communities: many nations share a print-language (Spanish America, the "Anglo-Saxon family"), and many post-colonial states (especially in Africa) have populations where only a tiny fraction actually uses the "national" print-language.
- This sets up Anderson's next analytical move (not in our excerpt): turning to the American republics (1776–1838) as the first self-conscious national/republican models, since they can't be explained by print-language alone.

# 2. HANNAH ARENDT — "The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man" (Ch.9, *Origins of Totalitarianism*)

This is a long, two-part chapter. Structurally it has two major sections: **I. "The Nation of Minorities" and the Stateless People** and **II. The Perplexities of the Rights of Man.**

## Opening framing
- Arendt opens with August 4, 1914 as an unrepeatable rupture — WWI "exploded the European comity of nations beyond repair" in a way nothing before had. She describes a chain reaction nobody could stop: hyperinflation wiped out the propertied middle class in a way no prior monetary crisis had; unemployment stopped being a working-class-only problem and seized whole nations; civil wars in the interwar "uneasy peace" were bloodier than their predecessors AND produced mass migrations of people who (unlike refugees from earlier religious wars) were welcomed nowhere and assimilated nowhere.
- Central formula: once people left their homeland they became **homeless**; once they left their state they became **stateless**; once deprived of human rights they became **rightless** — "the scum of the earth."

## SECTION I: The "Nation of Minorities" and the Stateless People

### The structural problem with the post-WWI settlement
- The Paris Peace Treaties tried to solve the nationality problem in Eastern/Southern Europe by creating new nation-states + minority treaties, but this was built on a false premise: that nation-states require homogeneity and "rootedness in soil" — conditions Eastern Europe simply didn't have (unlike Western Europe's more settled national traditions).
- The Treaties lumped multiple peoples into single states, designated some as the ruling "state people," and created a residual category called "minorities" — with the League of Nations, not the states themselves, nominally responsible for protecting minority rights.
- This created **two classes of inhabitants within the same state**: full citizens of the "state people," and minorities who needed special international protection just to have basic rights. This was the "worst factor" — some new states had nationally frustrated minority populations reaching up to 50% of the total population.

### Denationalization as a political weapon
- A "completely new element of disintegration" entered postwar Europe: **denationalization became a powerful weapon of totalitarian politics.** Once states discovered they could strip citizenship from unwanted populations, persecuting governments could impose their own values even on their opponents/victims, because there was no international mechanism to stop it.
- Chilling detail: the Nazi SS newspaper *Schwarze Korps* stated explicitly in 1938 that if the world wasn't yet convinced Jews were "scum of the earth," it soon would be once large numbers of unidentifiable, undocumented, stateless beggars crossed borders everywhere — i.e., statelessness *itself*, once mass-produced, functioned as proof of the persecutors' propaganda.
- The very phrase "human rights" became, for everyone involved (victims, persecutors, and bystanders alike), a marker either of hopeless idealism or of "fumbling feeble-minded hypocrisy."

### The failure of the Minority Treaties and League of Nations
- Minorities never trusted the League — it was composed of national statesmen whose sympathies lay with the aggrieved "state peoples," not the minorities.
- The Minority Treaties, meant as protection, were reinterpreted by their own framers as being about minorities' *duties* to the states — infuriating the minorities further.
- Minorities eventually organized their own "Congress of Organized National Groups" (deliberately using the forbidden word "national" that the peace negotiators had studiously avoided). This body was dominated by two groups present across many states: **Germans and Jews.** When in 1933 the Jewish delegation demanded the Congress protest Nazi treatment of German Jews, other delegations (many themselves antisemitic) refused solidarity — the Jewish delegation walked out, and the Congress collapsed into insignificance.
- Key point: the *real* significance of Minority Treaties lies not in their practical application (largely a failure) but in the fact that they were internationally guaranteed at all — establishing, for the first time on this scale, the idea that "the minority" is a permanent institutional category requiring external guarantee, rather than a temporary anomaly.
- The Minority Treaties, in plain language, admitted something devastating: only nationals could be full citizens; only people of the same national origin fully enjoyed legal protection; people of different nationality needed exceptional law until/unless fully assimilated. This was the tacit logic that WWII's postwar planners tried to escape via mass population "repatriation" (i.e., forced transfer) rather than genuinely solving the underlying problem.

### Statelessness as THE central 20th-century political phenomenon
- Arendt calls statelessness "the newest mass phenomenon in contemporary history" and "the most symptomatic group in contemporary politics."
- Groups of stateless people, in rough chronological order: the *Heimatlosen*/*apatrides* produced by the 1919 Peace Treaties and the dissolution of Austria-Hungary; then postwar refugees denationalized by revolutionary governments — millions of Russians, hundreds of thousands of Armenians, thousands of Hungarians, hundreds of thousands of Germans, over half a million Spaniards.
- Mass denationalization was something **entirely new** — it presupposed a state structure that, even if not yet fully totalitarian, would tolerate no opposition and would rather lose its own citizens than harbor dissent.
- The terminology itself deteriorated over time: "stateless" at least acknowledged these people had lost government protection and needed international legal safeguarding. The later, postwar term "displaced persons" was deliberately invented to erase/ignore the existence of statelessness as a legal-political problem altogether.
- **Key irony Arendt highlights**: nontotalitarian/liberal democracies, despite good intentions, shied away from mass repatriation — with the paradoxical result that the number of stateless people was, twelve years after WWII's end, *larger than ever.*
- The right of asylum — the oldest, most sacred remnant of the Rights of Man in international relations, dating to the beginnings of regulated political life — was effectively abolished by the sheer scale of the stateless influx (asylum was designed as an unofficial practice for handling exceptional individual cases, not millions of people).
- Naturalization also failed as a solution: European civil services simply could not process mass naturalization applications; countries began instead to *cancel* earlier naturalizations, deepening the crisis (this itself created a new vulnerable class: naturalized citizens now living in fear of being stripped of status too).

### The stateless person and the paradox of criminality
- One of Arendt's most famous and disturbing observations: it became *easier* to strip an innocent person of legal status than someone who had committed a crime. A criminal, paradoxically, retains a "legal personality" — a right to trial, a lawyer, a defined sentence, protections. The stateless person, having committed no crime, has *no* such protections and is entirely at the mercy of arbitrary police power.
- Practical implication: a stateless person could actually *improve* their situation by committing a minor crime, because this would grant them the "recognized exception" status of being a criminal under law — with all the (comparatively) greater legal protections that status carries, versus the total rightlessness of just existing as "the anomaly for whom the general law does not provide."
- The police, in dealing with the stateless, became for the first time in Western Europe an authority acting *independently of government and law* — no longer merely enforcing law but ruling directly, with power growing in direct proportion to the influx of refugees. Arendt links this directly to the danger of the police state.
- Chilling cross-national detail: totalitarian and non-totalitarian countries' police forces developed close informal cooperation (Gestapo/GPU liaising with Western police) around handling stateless populations — "an independent foreign policy of the police," functioning even when official government policy was anti-fascist (e.g., French police-Gestapo cooperation continuing cordially even under Léon Blum's anti-German Popular Front government).

### Closing move of Section I
- Nation-states cannot survive the collapse of legal equality before the law. Without it, the nation dissolves into "an anarchic mass of over- and under-privileged individuals" and laws become "rights and privileges" — a contradiction of the nation-state's own founding logic. Arendt frames the rise of statelessness as a **curse that attaches itself to every state founded in the image of the nation-state**, containing "the germs of a deadly sickness."

## SECTION II: The Perplexities of the Rights of Man

### The core paradox
- The 18th-century Declaration of the Rights of Man was meant to establish that Man himself, not God or tradition, is the source of law — rights that are "inalienable," requiring no higher authority, because Man is both source and goal.
- BUT — from the start there's a hidden, unresolved tension: the "people's" sovereignty (source of these rights) was *immediately* fused with **national** sovereignty. Rights of Man and rights of the citizen/national were treated as basically identical, because it seemed self-evident (post-French Revolution) that "the people," conceived on the model of a family of nations, was the image of man. Human rights were thus never actually independent of nationality — they were only ever *guaranteed* through national citizenship.

### When the fiction collapsed
- The trouble became visible only when a growing number of people (minorities, then the stateless) suddenly existed whose elementary rights were as unprotected in the middle of "civilized" Europe as they might have been "in the heart of Africa" (Arendt's own period-typical framing).
- The Rights of Man were defined as "inalienable" precisely because they were supposed to be independent of any government — but it turned out that the moment people actually lacked a government, **no authority was left to protect them and no institution was willing to guarantee them.** The abstraction "human being as such" turned out to carry no actual protective force.
- Minorities and stateless people themselves did NOT trust abstract "human rights" — they overwhelmingly sought reintegration into a *national* community instead, because they correctly intuited that only national rights had ever actually functioned in practice.

### What, precisely, was lost? Arendt's answer
This is one of the most important theoretical moves in the whole chapter, and likely a strong exam target.

- Arendt insists: **the calamity of the rightless is not that they are deprived of life, liberty, or the pursuit of happiness, nor of equality before the law or freedom of opinion** — these are all things that even citizens can lose under specific circumstances (a soldier in war is deprived of the right to life; a criminal, of freedom; all citizens in emergency, of the pursuit of happiness) without this constituting a "loss of human rights" as such.
- The **actual** loss is something more fundamental: **the loss of a place in the world that makes opinions significant and actions effective** — i.e., the loss not of *specific* rights but of **a community willing and able to guarantee any rights whatsoever.**
- This crystallizes into her single most quoted formulation: humanity discovered, through the existence of the stateless, **"the right to have rights"** — the right to belong to an organized community where one is judged by one's actions and opinions.
- Crucial distinction: the rightless are not deprived of the right to *think* whatever they please, but of the right to *opinion* (i.e., to have one's thinking matter in a shared public world); not deprived of freedom, but of the right to *action* (to have one's actions be effective, consequential, part of a common world).
- Man, Arendt argues, can lose all so-called Rights of Man **without losing his essential human dignity** — dignity is not erased by rightlessness. **Only the loss of a polity itself expels a person from humanity** (in the political, not biological, sense).

### The slavery comparison — and why it's inadequate as a precedent
- Arendt makes a pointed comparison: even slaves, historically, "belonged" to some kind of human community — their labor was needed, used, exploited, which kept them, paradoxically, "within the pale of humanity." Being a slave meant having a distinctive (if terrible) place in society — more than the "abstract nakedness of being human and nothing but human."
- The stateless/rightless of the 20th century are worse off than slaves in this specific structural sense: nobody needs them, nobody exploits them, nobody even wants to oppress them — they are simply *superfluous.* "Their plight is not that they are not equal before the law, but that no law exists for them; not that they are oppressed, but that nobody wants even to oppress them." Only in the final stage of a long process does their right to life itself become threatened — and only once they become perfectly "superfluous" with no one to "claim" them. (This directly anticipates the extermination camps: Arendt notes the Nazis first stripped Jews of all legal status and cut them off from the world of the living via ghettos/camps, carefully testing that no country would claim these people, BEFORE setting the gas chambers into motion — a condition of total rightlessness was manufactured before the right to live was challenged.)

### Burke vs. the French Revolution — Arendt's engagement
- Arendt gives real (if qualified) credit to Edmund Burke's conservative critique of the French Declaration. Burke argued that rights spring "from within the nation" as an "entailed inheritance" transmitted like life itself (e.g., the "rights of an Englishman," not abstract universal Man) — not from natural law, divine command, or an abstraction like "mankind."
- Arendt says the historical record has ironically vindicated Burke's *pragmatic* point (though not necessarily his conservative politics): the survivors of extermination/concentration/internment camps, and even the "comparatively happy" stateless, discovered by hard experience that the abstract "nakedness of being nothing but human" was their **greatest danger**, not their protection. This is precisely why the stateless clung so fiercely and "all the more desperately" to their nationality once it was gone — because only their national past, with its "entailed inheritance," seemed to attest that they still belonged to the civilized world at all.

### Two further philosophical moves worth noting for exam purposes
1. **The "world government" caveat**: Arendt anticipates the objection that a world government might solve this by universalizing rights-guarantee. She's skeptical — not because it's impossible, but because it might differ drastically from the idealistic version hoped for by "idealistic-minded organizations," and raises the deeper danger that a "highly organized and mechanized humanity" could one day conclude, quite democratically (by majority decision), that it would be better to liquidate certain parts of itself. She links this to the old philosophical problem (going back to Plato) of what happens once there's no transcendent measure ("Not man, but a god, must be the measure of all things") and law collapses into "whatever is good for" the largest unit.
2. **The alien as disruptive figure**: Arendt closes with a reflection on why "highly developed" political communities (ancient city-states or modern nation-states) so often insist on ethnic homogeneity — they are trying to eliminate the natural/given differences that disturb the political order's presumption of equality. The "alien" is frightening precisely because they represent individuality/difference *as such* — a reminder of the limits of human artifice and the limits of human-made equality (equality, for Arendt, is not natural/given but a *product* of political organization — "we are not born equal; we become equal as members of a group on the strength of our decision to guarantee ourselves mutually equal rights"). The great danger of the stateless is twofold: their sheer numbers threaten political/civilizational order the way natural disaster once did (but now from *within* civilization, not external barbarian invasion) — a "globally interrelated civilization" can produce its own barbarians from within by forcing millions of people into the conditions of savages.

# 3. GIORGIO AGAMBEN — "What Is a Camp?" (from *Means Without End*)

**IMPORTANT CAVEAT REPEATED**: this is NOT the syllabus-listed *State of Exception* chapter (pp.1–31). It's a shorter, related essay covering overlapping ground (the camp, the state of exception, sovereignty) but is a distinct text with its own structure. Treat this summary as background/supplementary until the actual assigned chapter is obtained.

## Agamben's core methodological move
- Rather than starting from the *events* that took place in the camps (the atrocities) and working backward to try to explain them morally/legally, Agamben deliberately reverses the direction: he asks **"What is a camp? What is its political-juridical structure? How could such events have taken place there?"**
- His central provocation: the camp should not be understood as a **historical anomaly belonging to the past** (however horrific), but as **"the hidden matrix and *nomos* of the political space in which we still live."** I.e., the camp isn't behind us — it's the secret organizing principle of contemporary politics.

## Origins of the camp — legal-historical argument
- Historians debate whether the first camps were the Spanish *campos de concentraciones* in Cuba (1896, suppressing colonial insurrection) or the British concentration camps for Boers (early 20th century). Agamben says what matters isn't which came first but the shared structural feature: **both extended a state of exception, normally tied to colonial war, to an entire civilian population.**
- Crucial claim: camps were **not** born out of ordinary law, and **not** a development/extension of prison law. They were born out of **the state of exception and martial law.**
- This is even clearer with the Nazi *Lager*: its juridical foundation was NOT ordinary criminal or prison law but the ***Schutzhaft*** ("protective custody") — a Prussian-derived legal instrument that let the state take individuals into custody regardless of any actual criminal behavior, purely to preempt threats to state security. The *Schutzhaft*'s own origin traces back to 19th-century Prussian laws on the "state of siege" (1851, extended to Germany 1871) and "protection of personal freedom" (1850) — both widely applied during WWI.

## The central irony of the *Schutzhaft*
- The term literally means "protective" custody — protection of freedom. But the freedom being "protected" was freedom *from the suspension of law itself* that characterizes a state of emergency. What's new and disturbing: this originally *temporary* emergency institution became **dissolved by the very state of exception it was founded on, and was then allowed to continue operating permanently, under supposedly normal circumstances.**

## Agamben's key formula: **"The camp is the space that opens up when the state of exception starts to become the rule."**
- In the camp, the state of exception — originally just a *temporary* suspension of the rule of law — acquires a **permanent spatial arrangement** that remains constantly outside the normal legal order.
- Historical illustration: when Himmler created Dachau in March 1933, it was immediately entrusted to the SS and, via the *Schutzhaft*, placed entirely outside the jurisdiction of both criminal law AND prison law — it never had anything to do with either. Dachau and the camps that followed (Sachsenhausen, Buchenwald, Lichtenberg) remained "virtually always operative" even as prisoner numbers fluctuated (dropping to 7,500 in 1935–37, before Jewish deportations began) — the camp *as such* had become a **permanent reality**, independent of how many people were in it at any given moment.

## The paradoxical spatial status of the camp
- The camp is "a piece of territory placed outside the normal juridical order" — but crucially, it is **not simply an external space.**
- Agamben leans on the etymology of "exception" (*ex-capere*, literally "taken outside"): what is excluded in the camp is **"captured outside"** — meaning it is *included* precisely *by virtue of* its exclusion. The camp doesn't exist outside the law's reach; the law's reach *includes* the camp precisely as its exception.
- Therefore: sovereign power is founded on the ability to *decide* on the state of exception — and the camp is the physical structure where that state of exception becomes **permanently realized**, rather than a temporary emergency measure.

## Arendt connection (explicit citation)
- Agamben directly invokes Arendt's observation that what becomes visible in the camps is the core principle underlying totalitarian domination, one which "common sense stubbornly refuses to admit": **the principle that anything is possible.** It's only because the camp constitutes a total space of exception — where law is *completely* suspended — that "everything is truly possible" there.
- This is why, for Agamben, understanding the camp's specific political-juridical structure is a precondition for understanding the "incredible events" that occurred inside it — without this structural understanding, the atrocities remain "entirely unintelligible."

## The reframed central question
- Agamben insists the "correct question" about camp horrors is NOT the hypocritical "how could human beings do this to other human beings" — but rather the more honest and useful question: **through what specific juridical procedures and political devices could human beings be so completely stripped of rights that literally anything done to them ceased to register as a crime?**
- Connects directly to citizenship: camp inhabitants moved in "a zone of indistinction" between outside/inside, exception/rule, licit/illicit — and crucially, Jewish inmates had *already* been stripped of citizenship rights by the Nuremberg Laws, then fully denationalized at the moment of the "Final Solution." **Only once inhabitants are stripped of all political status and reduced to "naked life" does the camp become "the most absolute biopolitical space ever realized"** — a space where power confronts nothing but pure biological life, without mediation.
- This is the point where, for Agamben, "the *homo sacer* becomes indistinguishable from the citizen" — the camp is politics becoming pure biopolitics.

## The expansion of the concept: camps are not just Nazi camps
- Agamben insists we must recognize a "camp," in his structural sense, **"virtually every time such a structure is created, regardless of the nature of the crimes committed in it and regardless of the denomination and specific topography it might have."**
- His examples (important for exam purposes — these show how far he pushes the concept):
 - The 1991 Bari soccer stadium where Italian police temporarily herded Albanian illegal immigrants before deportation
 - The Vichy-era cycle-racing track (Vél d'Hiv) used to round up Jews before handing them to Germans
 - The refugee camp near the Spanish border where poet Antonio Machado died in 1939
 - The *zones d'attente* in French international airports, where asylum seekers are detained (he specifically names the Hotel Arcade near Paris airport as an "apparently anodyne place" that is structurally a camp)
 - Even, provocatively, **certain outskirts of postindustrial cities and U.S. gated communities**, which he says are "beginning to look like camps" in the sense that naked life and political life enter a zone of absolute indeterminacy there, at least in certain moments.
- The common thread in all these: an ostensibly ordinary/anodyne place delimits a space where the **normal rule of law is suspended**, and whether atrocities happen or not depends not on law but on **the arbitrary civility/ethical sense of whoever is acting as temporary sovereign** (e.g., the police).

## The theoretical payoff: the camp and the structure of the nation-state itself
- Agamben, following Carl Schmitt's classic definition, says the nation-state is structured around three elements: **territory, order, birth** (localization/*Ortung*, order/*Ordnung*, and the automatic inscription of biological birth into national belonging).
- His claim: the modern crisis is not a rupture in the *territory* or *order* elements per se, but at the site where **naked life gets inscribed into them** — i.e., the mechanism that turns mere biological "birth" into "nation" is what's broken.
- The camp is **"the new hidden regulator of the inscription of life in the order"** — or, put more starkly, it is the sign of the system's inability to function *without transforming itself into a lethal machine.*
- Important historical-timing point: camps appeared at the **same time** as new citizenship/denationalization laws (not just the Nuremberg Laws, but denaturalization laws passed by almost all European states, including France, between 1915 and 1933). This is not coincidence for Agamben — the state of exception, once a temporary suspension, becomes "a new and stable spatial arrangement inhabited by that naked life that increasingly cannot be inscribed into the order."
- Final formula: **"The camp is the fourth and inseparable element that has been added to and has broken up the old trinity of nation (birth), state, and territory."**

## The former Yugoslavia example (his closing case study)
- Agamben argues the camps that reappeared in the former Yugoslavia (writing in 1994) are NOT simply a repetition of the older European nation-state-formation process (new ethnic/territorial arrangements). Rather, they represent an **irreparable rupture of the old *nomos*** and a dislocation of populations along "entirely new lines of flight."
- His most disturbing example: **camps of ethnic rape.** He argues that even the Nazis never attempted the "Final Solution" via forced impregnation, because the principle of *birth* (linking bodies to national inscription) was still functioning, however monstrously, under Nazism. In the Yugoslav camps of ethnic rape, that principle of birth is itself now "adrift" — actively weaponized/broken rather than simply exploited — suggesting an even more radical dislocation.
- Closing line: **"The camp, which is now firmly settled inside [the city], is the new biopolitical *nomos* of the planet."**

# 4. ÉTIENNE BALIBAR — *Citizenship*, Ch.1 & Ch.2 (pp.1–28)

## CHAPTER 1: "Democracy and Citizenship: An Antinomic Relationship"

### The book's opening problem
- Balibar insists the relationship between citizenship and democracy is **not simply reciprocal** — despite the book being titled *Citizenship*, he does NOT think citizenship straightforwardly "trumps" or subordinates democracy as a secondary qualifier.
- He explicitly refuses to resolve the question of "lexical priority" (a term he borrows from Rawls) between citizenship and democracy — this is a real, high-stakes battleground between "republican"/neo-republican and "democratic" (liberal or social) traditions in political philosophy, as shown by thinkers like Jacques Rancière and Miguel Abensour.
- His actual position: **democracy — or better, the "democratic paradox" (borrowing Chantal Mouffe's term) — is the determining feature that makes the institution of citizenship *problematic* in the first place.**

### The concept of "antinomy" — central methodological term
- Balibar uses "antinomic" deliberately, drawing on a Western philosophical tradition emphasizing two things:
 1. A **permanent tension** between the positive and negative — between construction and destruction.
 2. **The persistence of a problem that can never be "definitively" resolved nor utterly eliminated.**
- His working hypothesis: at the very heart of the institution of citizenship there is a **contradiction with regard to democracy** that continually re-emerges throughout history. He wants to trace the "moments of a dialectical process" across history — from Aristotle to Marx, with detours through Spinoza — showing how theory and practice have been (or could be) joined.

### Historical arc of the citizenship/democracy pairing (Aristotle → Marx)
- **Aristotle** (*Politics*, Book III): argues all political regimes involving citizens as "office-holders" for either definite or indefinite terms (*aoristos archē*) will always contain an ineliminable *democratic* element — but Aristotle's own goal was to *mitigate* this risk by transforming democracy into what he calls, in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "timocracy."
- **Spinoza** (*Tractatus Politicus*, unfinished, 1677): reverses Aristotle's direction of concern — for Spinoza democracy isn't a discrete "type" of regime so much as the built-in *tendency* within any monarchical or aristocratic regime for power to devolve toward "the multitude."
- **Young Marx** (*Critique of Hegel's "Philosophy of Right,"* 1843): goes further still, explicitly holding that democracy — or "legislative authority" — is the "truth of all constitutions."
- **Rancière** (contemporary): returns to this thesis, showing that no regime can ever fully eliminate the risk posed by needing to secure the consent of its people, who can always "decide to obey — or not."
- **Antonio Negri** (1999): sees this as central to an "affirmative" theory of the *constituent power* of "the multitude" — with states that monopolize power instruments continually trying to replace this democratic capacity with mere *constituted* power.
- Balibar's own move: he wants to interpret this whole tradition **dialectically**, differently from all the above — arguing that the antinomy lodged at citizenship's heart has, in its successive historical forms, been **the actual motor for the transformation of the political institution itself.** This means "democratic citizenship" can only ever refer to a **persistent, unresolvable problem** — even though history periodically produces moments where thinkers *declare* the problem "finally solved" (as Marx claimed) or a "lost treasure" rediscovered (as Arendt claimed — note the direct link to our other reading here).

### The four "aspects of the dialectic" Balibar plans to explore (roadmap for the whole book, useful for orienting yourself in later chapters)
1. The ancient Greek "constitution of citizenship" (*politeia*) — preceding the modern civil society/state split.
2. The legacy of the bourgeois revolutions and their appeal to **"equaliberty" (*égaliberté*)** — a key Balibar coinage meaning the inseparability of liberty and equality — within modern *national* citizenship, and the tension between insurrection and constitution, and between different theories/practices of *representation*.
3. The internal contradictions of "**social citizenship**" as constituted within the "social-national state" (his preferred term over "Welfare State"/*État-Providence*/*Sozialstaat*) — a real democratic achievement historically, but one that paradoxically becomes an impediment to further progress. Specifically: the relationship between citizenship and social exclusion, and between citizenship and civil conflict.
4. The **neo-liberal reaction** to the crisis of the social-national state — Balibar wants to examine (via Wendy Brown's interpretation) neoliberal governance as a process of **"de-democratization of democracy,"** which may or may not be reversible. His own view: this is an expression of the *destructive* character built into citizenship's antinomies. His planned conclusion superimposes "de-democratization" against **"the democratization of democracy" as a recourse for citizenship.**

## CHAPTER 2: "Politeia"

### Setting up the problem: can "citizenship" be universal without being Eurocentric?
- Balibar acknowledges the era of Western nations seeing themselves as civilizational center is over, and cites postcolonial critiques (e.g., Partha Chatterjee) showing classical political philosophy's categories don't simply apply to "most of the world."
- Nonetheless, he wants to salvage a certain **universal character** for political constructions emerging from the Western tradition — but insists this must be **"intensive" universality** (turning political institutions into tools for universalizing social relationships, removing barriers between citizens, toppling domination) rather than **"extensive"** universality (a single model spreading everywhere, all-encompassing).

### The untranslatability of *politeia* — key linguistic/conceptual point
- "Constitution" (in the modern European sense — *constituzione*, *Verfassung*, *syntagma*) tends to emphasize the construction of a unified body politic. But Balibar argues the deeper philosophical resource is the ancient Greek term ***politeia*** — which the Romans translated as *res publica* and the British classical tradition translated (at different points) as "polity" then "commonwealth."
- He cites Francis Wolff's definition (from the *Dictionary of Untranslatables*): the Greek *polis* is **neither a nation, nor a state, nor a society** — it's the identity of the sphere of power (what we'd call "the state") WITH the sphere of community (what we'd call "society"), and it's to this fused unity, not to "the nation," that individuals feel affectively bound. From this, *politeia* can mean EITHER (a) the subjective bond of the citizen (*politēs*) to the *polis* — i.e., how the community distributes membership — OR (b) the objective organization of governmental functions — i.e., "constitution" in our sense.

### Aristotle's THREE-STAGE definition of *politeia* — likely a high-value exam item
Balibar lays this out very precisely, citing Aristotle's *Politics* Book III:

1. **First (most general/abstract, 1275a30)**: a *politeia* exists wherever there are "citizens" and therefore "city law" — where individuals constituting the *polis* rotate through positions of power exercising an ***aoristos archē*** — an **"indefinite office"**: indefinite in duration, but also indefinite in its object/modalities — which makes these citizens the *kurioi* ("sovereigns"/"masters") of their own community.
2. **Second (the principal, "discriminating" definition, 1277a25)**: a *politeia* exists between those who can, depending on circumstance, alternately **give orders (*archein*) or receive them (*archesthai*)** — ruling and being ruled in turn, rotating freely among positions of power. This is the crucial "reciprocity" definition.
3. **Third and final (1282a25, repeated Book V 1301a25)**: a *politeia* exists where powers/offices (*archai*) are **"proportionally" (*isos*) distributed** among citizens according to their competencies/capabilities, and in accordance with **law (*nomos*)**. This expresses Aristotle's belief/hope that the city, as a concrete stable form, can achieve its natural goal — the common good, the possibility of a "good life."

### The tension embedded in "indefinite office" (*aoristos archē*)
- This idea is linked to Aristotle's most radical argument: that the essence/foundation of any *political* regime lies in the **sovereignty of its own citizens** — it would be contradictory for power not ultimately to belong to those for whose benefit it exists.
- This thesis has been perpetually attacked by authoritarian traditions (arguing "the multitude" can't govern itself) and periodically reaffirmed by Machiavelli, Rousseau, young Marx, Tocqueville, and Arendt (again — direct cross-reading link).
- Aristotle gave this content via two concrete responsibilities for citizens: ***boulein*** (deliberation/decision in popular assemblies) and ***krinein*** (exercise of legal/judicial duties).

### The reciprocity principle and its radical LIMITATION
- The idea of alternating governed/governing (*to dunasthai archein te kai archestai*) — the notion that "by obeying we learn to command, and by exercising authority we learn to obey" — constitutes, for Aristotle, the very virtue (*arete*) of a citizen. Balibar links this to what would later be called (in more Roman language) "equal liberty" — not just as legal *status* but in actual *practice*.
- Balibar cites Herodotus's Persian Prince Otanes, who states the negative/polemical version: "without an equal distribution of power, I wish to neither command nor obey" — i.e., either democracy or anarchy.
- **BUT** — and this is the crucial critical move — this reciprocity is IMMEDIATELY accompanied by a **radical limitation of citizenship**: for Aristotle and his contemporaries, reciprocity can only exist between those who are **equal *by nature***. This means the core of "the political" contains, built in from the start, **a mechanism for discrimination based on anthropological difference**: gender, age, and the difference between manual and intellectual capacity — specifically insofar as this last difference was used to **justify slavery**. (Balibar notes Aristotle knew perfectly well that many actual Athenian citizens were peasants/artisans, but he retained — following Plato even further — the aristocratic view that manual labor was inherently "servile.")
- Balibar's summary judgment: this "opens up not just a gap, but an abyss" — the principle establishing *demos*'s intensive/qualitative universality *simultaneously* involves a seemingly insurmountable **quantitative exclusion.** And modern universalism's transformation of "equal liberty" into the "rights of man and citizen" within a *national* framework has, Balibar argues, **aggravated rather than relieved this tension** — even though it also enabled real emancipatory struggles (women, laborers becoming/re-becoming citizens). The structural cost: those who remain excluded are actively **"produced"** by disciplinary/institutional mechanisms as "imperfect human beings," as "abnormals" or monsters at humanity's margins.
- He invokes Rancière's term for those structurally excluded even from among nominal citizens: **the "share-less"** — for whom the chance to obey will always outnumber the chance to lead or initiate.
- Balibar's own aspirational move (again citing Arendt's "right to rights," and directly parallel to Arendt's own argument in our other reading): true *politeia* shouldn't just distribute reciprocity on the basis of a "given" pre-existing equality but should actively **widen the scope of equality**, "producing it as a fiction," continually transgressing limits imposed by "nature" (which is actually just tradition in disguise).

### The tension around *nomos* (law) as proportional distribution
- A second, related but distinct tension (Balibar cautions against conflating it simplistically with the reciprocity/exclusion tension above): Aristotle's third definition of *politeia* (law as *proportional* distribution of offices) seems to *retract* some of what was conceded via *aoristos archē* — it limits the sovereignty of the *demos* in the name of rational "common good" and good government.
- Using Rancière again: it's not just that citizens don't have an *equal* share in decision-making power — there must necessarily, structurally, be those who are "share-less," expected to be passive rather than active. This, Balibar argues, is **the price paid for consensus/equilibrium (*homonoia*) — the price for replacing open conflict (class struggle broadly conceived, majority/minority struggle) with consensus, and relegating conflict outside the public sphere.**
- BUT — crucially — repressed conflict "will always re-emerge... on new battlefields." Balibar floats the idea (via Machiavelli, whom he says is "increasingly relevant" today at both national and *global* levels) that **the acceptance/recognition of conflict within the public sphere may actually be a necessary condition for institutional equilibrium itself** — at least for a "dynamic" rather than static equilibrium.

### Autonomy vs. Autarchy of the political — an important conceptual distinction
- Balibar draws a sharp distinction between two things often collapsed together:
 - **Autonomy of the political**: the idea that the *polis*/*politeia* operates on a "plane of immanence" — a radically secular system of relationships citizens establish among *themselves*, with NO subjugation to any transcendent authority (not the Platonic Idea of the Good, not Comte's "Great Being," not Kant's "community of ends"). The only overarching principle is the community itself.
 - **Autarchy of the polis**: the (separable, though historically fused) idea of **relative closure/self-sufficiency** — isolation from the outside world/*cosmos*/*oikoumenē*, AND (a second sense of autarchy) *economic* independence from material conditions of production/reproduction, which historically meant relegating labor, sexuality, maternity, and child-rearing to a "domestic sphere of internal exclusion" — historically corresponding to **patriarchy and slavery.**
- Balibar's key move: he thinks a critical (esp. Marxist) tradition has too quickly treated autonomy and autarchy as simply "two sides of the same idealization" — but he thinks it's worth trying to pull them apart. With globalization, he argues, the two historically distinct senses of autarchy have themselves "fused together" — meaning autarchic limitations of the *politeia* have "irreparably collapsed as borders." No *polis*, in any metaphorical sense, can construct itself within such closed limits any longer — it can only exist as an **"open city."**
- His conclusion here: the autonomy of the political — as an autonomization of collective action, immanent within the "body politic" — can only mean something today if we **renounce, once and for all, the myth of autarchy** (and its accompanying myths: autochthony, "untranslatable" cultural specificity) — while still trying to preserve rights, obligations, distribution of powers, and collective self-government within an *open*, non-autarchic community.

### *Politeia* and the "Withering Away of the State" (section heading)
- Balibar poses a genuinely open question (not a resolved claim): would renewed examination of the dominant nation-state form of citizenship **reopen** the ancient *politeia* dialectic — reviving the tension between the "pressure of equal liberty" (reciprocal rights recognition) and the demands of regulating social conflict (self-limitation of power)?
- **Negative argument**: Europe underwent a long historical cycle (imperial → urban → state forms) during which citizenship was repeatedly lost and reconstituted by fusing with nation-building. Balibar cites Max Weber's analysis of the medieval/Renaissance city (*Stadt*) as a form of "illegitimate domination" — power with no transcendental guarantee, permanently exposed to insurrection — as a historical exception where ancient-style citizenship briefly survived. But the overall cycle subordinated citizenship to the *language and function of the state*. Balibar cites Nietzsche's line from *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, "Ich, der Staat, bin das Volk" ("I, the state, am the people") as a bitterly ironic marker of this — written, pointedly, at the very moment European imperial powers were carving up the world at the Congress of Berlin.
- Modern constitutions (per Negri's critique) have redefined "constituent power" not as insurrectional power but as a **state function** — e.g., merely the right to revise the constitution within pre-set limits, or to periodically vote out office-holders.
- This produces genuine **alienation** (in the full philosophical sense) even within the "freest" states — reaching its peak in the modern European "social-national state" as a **"community of passive citizens."** Balibar notes such welfare states, where they existed, were nonetheless the *product* of age-old confrontation with active/insurrectional social movements — meaning conflict *did* get built permanently into the core of the formalized state, even if only partially.
- Balibar's summary line: in the extreme, the state configuration of the political contains no "democracy" in the pure/ideal sense — but there can always be a **process of *democratization*** — which he suggests may in truth matter more than any static ideal of "democracy" itself.
- **Note of caution/limit**: he flags that revolutionary labor movements historically "always started in internationalism only to end up in nationalism" — even anti-colonial solidarity movements didn't produce lasting structural change. He reads the EU as a possible **prefiguration** of a coming form: "**statism without a state**," operating under the name **"governance"** — nominally sovereign "citizens" being asked to perform a "constituent" role for a body (the EU) that is itself only "the ghost of a state," lacking any real element of collective identification. He warns this vacillation, so far, hasn't sparked a genuine democratic renewal, nor a simple return to older autarchic/communal forms — instead producing "a period of extreme danger for the democratic tradition" that could last a long time (though not, he stresses, a *fatal* obstacle to eventual renewal).

### "Civil Society": The New Location of the *Politeia*? — his critical assessment
- Balibar considers, then substantially critiques, "civil society" as the proposed new site for a post-state *politeia* — a position where Marxists/post-Marxists and liberal thinkers have found unusual (if only verbal) agreement (citing Cohen and Arato, 1992).
- He also addresses **Hardt and Negri's concept of "the multitude"** as a possible radicalization of the civil-society answer — but argues it's not simply that, because their concept builds in a *division* between the multitude's constituent force and the social forms imposed by capitalism (making it a **decomposition**, not autonomization, of civil society); because for Hardt/Negri the "empire" the multitude resists includes the *state itself* (state and civil society aren't external to each other in their model); and because they locate the multitude's power in an "ontology of productive forces" / biopolitics *underneath* civil society's actual institutions.
- Balibar's own **two specific weaknesses** with identifying *politeia*-to-come with "civil society":
 1. Civil society includes forces (above all **capitalist markets**, which have commodified not just material goods but *life itself* — care, culture) that aren't just undemocratic but **not even political** in the recovered sense of the term. What should be positively affirmed isn't "civil society" as a monolith but the **differences/tensions *between* tendencies within civil society** — specifically the ongoing confrontation between public-interest and private-interest logics.
 2. "Civil society" as a reference point tends to *exclude* supra-state institutional forms — global judicial/economic/environmental/health bodies (UN, WHO, International Criminal Courts, World Bank, IMF). Locating a genuine *politeia* within these would require **radical democratization** that remains, per Balibar, "very distant" — because it would require not just severing these bodies' current dependence on states, but a decisive step "beyond the Leviathan" (citing Marramao) toward **shared and relativized state sovereignty.**

### Closing line of the excerpt (Ch.2 ends on this note)
- Balibar acknowledges powerful forces will "stubbornly, even violently" resist any inversion of the transmission corridor between state *imperium* and international/global *auctoritas*. He ends without an answer, only a reflexive methodological point: "In the present moment, we can *ask* this question, but we cannot *answer* it. But history does not ask (itself) only questions that it can answer... Or maybe it can only answer them by posing them differently."

# CROSS-TEXT CONNECTIONS (useful synthesis for essay-style midterm questions)

- **Arendt ↔ Agamben**: Agamben explicitly cites Arendt's "anything is possible" principle as the key to understanding the camp. Both treat the camp/statelessness as revealing the hidden truth of sovereignty and the nation-state, not as an aberration from it.
- **Arendt ↔ Balibar**: Both use the phrase/concept "the right to have rights" (Arendt coins it; Balibar cites it directly in Ch.2). Both are wrestling with the same core problem: what happens to "universal" rights/citizenship claims when the concrete political community that would guarantee them is absent or exclusionary.
- **Anderson ↔ Arendt/Balibar**: Anderson explains HOW national imagining became historically possible (print-capitalism) — Arendt and Balibar then examine what happens when that national form breaks down (statelessness) or was always internally contradictory (Balibar's antinomy of citizenship/democracy, built-in exclusion via slavery/gender in Aristotle).
- **Agamben ↔ Balibar**: Both are interested in the *structural*, not merely contingent, relationship between sovereignty/law and exclusion — Agamben via the camp as "hidden matrix," Balibar via the *politeia*'s built-in "abyss" between intensive universality and quantitative exclusion (share-less citizens, slaves, women).
- **All four together**: they collectively interrogate the nation-state/citizenship nexus from complementary angles — Anderson (how the nation became imaginable), Balibar (the ancient philosophical roots of citizenship's internal contradiction with democracy), Arendt (what happens when the nation-state's promise of rights collapses for real people), Agamben (the camp as the structural, permanent underside of the nation-state's claim to inscribe "life" into "order").
